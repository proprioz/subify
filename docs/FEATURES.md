# Features and design decisions

Subify lets you follow your favourite video creators without an account, without being tracked, and without seeing content you didn't ask for: only the latest videos of the channels you chose. This document describes what the app does and why. Design decisions are numbered (D1, D2…): the code and the other documents refer to them.

## 1. Feed (Feed tab)

- **Latest uploads** of the followed channels, sorted by publication date (`published`), never by arrival order. No seen / unseen status, no history (D1).
- **Period headers** (Today, Yesterday, This week, Earlier this month, month, month + year) and a relative age on each card (“3 days ago”).
- **Format badge** V / S / L on each card; items the server could not classify (`unknown`) have none.
- **“Show in feed” filters**: videos, lives, Shorts, at least one on, all shown by default. They combine with the types chosen for each channel: an item shows only if both allow it. `unknown` items stay visible as long as “videos” is on.
- **Upcoming** (D13): premieres and lives not yet aired come first, under the “Upcoming” header, with “Upcoming” in place of the age. An “Upcoming” checkbox, checked by default, hides them.
- **Avatar strip** above the feed: “Options”, then “All” and one avatar per channel (from two channels), tap to filter. “Options” opens on Close / Search / Show / Sort (Sort from two channels).
- **Channel sort** (recently added, oldest, A→Z, Z→A), shared with the Channels tab and stored in `meta`.
- **Local search** in titles and channel names (`%` and `_` taken literally): a Dexie filter, with no network request.
- **Paged loading**, 15 at a time (`IonInfiniteScroll`).
- **Refresh**: a round button next to the title, and `IonRefresher` (pull to refresh). No automatic sync (D12). The app reads the sync stream of its groups (`/stream`, D16), page by page, **newest first**: it asks for the latest pass, then reads back from there to the last pass it has, so that after an absence the latest videos show first; a change older than one already written for the same video or channel is skipped. It reads back from that last pass rounded down to a multiple of 72 passes (12 hours at the 10-minute period): every device whose last refresh fell in the same 12 hours sends the same `since`, so the cursor doesn't tie two refreshes of a device together. The passes read again cost up to 12 hours of stream (about 0.8 MB at today's size, 0.4 MB on average) and change nothing. The groups of a page are asked for six at a time. The progress bar is a thin red line at the very top of the screen, over every page, so the refresh shows wherever the user goes; it advances with time (capped at 95 / 99 %, visible for at least 500 ms), and with the pages read when there are several. The icon spins while the refresh runs. If the sync fails, the subtitle shows the error in red until the next successful sync, and a toast says so on any page with a **Resume** button, which picks up where it stopped. A sync cut short by the app closing doesn't go on by itself (D12): at the next launch, a toast offers to resume it.
- **Feed freshness**: an “Updated X ago” subtitle (minutes, hours, then days).
- **Tapping a card copies its link** (D2): `navigator.clipboard` (falling back to a `textarea`), a vibration where `navigator.vibrate` exists (not on iOS), a “Link copied.” toast.
- **`⋯` menu**: Open in YouTube, Share… (Web Share), More from <channel>. Links open with `noopener,noreferrer`.
- **Deleted videos**: `at:deleted-entry` (WebSub) makes a **tombstone** in the channel's slot: the item keeps its id and is marked deleted; the next pass passes it on through the stream and the app greys out the card (“Unavailable”, not copyable). A video that disappears from a feed during polling is not marked deleted: windows of 15 entries would give false positives.
- **Thumbnails** kept on the device (D3): `mqdefault.jpg` (320×180), which the server keeps as WebP (about 7 KB). A channel added brings those of its last 7 days and upcoming videos, and so does a restored backup, or a return after more than the stream keeps, for every channel; a refresh then brings those of new videos from the thumbnail lots, after the stream, the most recent hour first, back 7 days for a device that was away, and only the hours it hasn't read. A new video shows at once, with a blurred picture drawn from its ThumbHash and a small download mark (tapping it says the thumbnail is on its way); the thumbnail then fades in, usually within the hour of the video. The device keeps them as long as the user chose in Settings, a month by default. A snapshot carries the ThumbHash of each video of the last 7 days, so a restored device shows them blurred at once, then sharp as their thumbnails arrive. A video without a ThumbHash (older, or from before the lots) shows a 16:9 placeholder that looks like a blurred picture, drawn on the device from the video's key: each video has its own, always the same, and nothing is stored or loaded. A refresh cut short in its thumbnails is offered again at the next launch, like the stream. A Short shows its vertical frame alone, over the same blurred picture in the frame's main colour, computed on the device, instead of the blurred enlargement the platform puts on each side.
- **Empty states** (no channel, nothing for the search or the channel, nothing yet) with “Add a channel” / “Restore a backup”.

## 2. Channels (Channels tab)

- **Channel list** with avatar, name and latest activity (“Last upload 2 days ago”), computed locally.
- **A channel the server couldn't read yet.** When YouTube's feeds failed for a channel with no videos in the catalog (typically one just added during an outage), its row reads “Refresh after 14:30” (“Refresh in a few minutes” once that time has come), with an ⓘ that says the last try failed and when the next one comes, and its feed shows the same line as its heading, the reason under it, in place of an empty list. These messages name neither the platform nor the app. The time comes sealed with the channel, in its snapshot entry and the sync stream (D16, `retryAt`): the server learns nothing more. It disappears with the channel's first videos.
- **A single field**, “Search or add a channel” (`src/components/ChannelSearch.tsx`). As soon as something is typed, one list replaces the page's, as a search box's suggestions: the followed channels the text matches, with “Following” (a tap opens their feed; a secret one only while the secret channels show), the channels the dictionary names like it, each with “Follow”, then the channel a likely `@handle` of the name names in the catalog, looked up blindly once typing pauses (600 ms) and shown as `@handle`, and last, for a name, the options, as chips under a heading (“Nothing found yet”, “Not listed?” under suggestions, “Not the one?” once a handle found a channel, “No channel found” once every chip found nothing): the name's likely `@handle`s (4 at most, `guessesOf`: the words run together, then with the device's language and “official” after them, then joined by `.` or `_`), under “Is it one of these on YouTube? The server sees the one you tap, not who you are.”, and what always works, under “Or look for it in YouTube: this leaves Subify and opens the YouTube app, or its website. There, tap Share, then Copy link, and come back here to paste it.”, which says before the tap that the user leaves the app: “Open YouTube” (YouTube's logo, and the ↗ of a link that leaves the app), which opens YouTube's own search for the name, filtered to channels, in YouTube's app when the system hands the link to it, else in a new tab, and “Paste link”, which reads the clipboard on the tap and looks the link up at once (a link to the channel or to one of its videos; with the clipboard refused, the field takes the focus, its text selected, and a toast says to paste there). A link, a `UC…` id has “Find this channel” instead (`isChannelInput`); a `@handle` typed is offered as the one chip. Under 3 letters, the list says that channels to add show from 3 letters. Each channel shows once, and a row keeps its place once followed: only its button turns to “Following”. “Follow” follows at once and opens no window: found blindly in the catalog as a typed link would be (`followOne`: its group's snapshot, then its thumbnails), or, when the catalog lacks it, put in the import queue, which adds it in the background (“Waiting to be added…”). A handle's chip, or Enter (the first chip left), is the only step that names the channel to the server: the user takes it, told what the server sees. It first finishes on the device what typing couldn't (the dictionary not downloaded yet, the likely handles kept back by the connection check), and asks the server to read that one handle on YouTube only when it isn't in the catalog; a channel found joins the same list, with “Follow”, and a handle that names none stays as a greyed chip with ✕; the answer is kept in memory, so following it asks nothing more, and leaving it leaves nothing on the device. While it runs, the row says “Looking for “…”…” on the device and “Checking YouTube for @…” on the server, and after 3 seconds “This can take a few seconds.” (the server answers after 8 seconds at least). A link that names no channel ends the list with “No channel found” and the same “Open YouTube” and “Paste link”, which find any channel. Nothing goes to the server in clear while typing, since each unknown channel costs the server a read online and a name typed would reach it letter by letter: a chip or Enter. No step searches YouTube by name (no Data API, no InnerTube): the server only reads the page of the handle tapped, and YouTube's own search is the user's visit to YouTube, which the Privacy page says.
- **Accepted links**: `/channel/UC…`, `@handle`, `/c/`, `/user/`, feed URL, bare id, and video links (`watch?v=`, `youtu.be`, `/shorts/`, `/live/`, D7). A channel id or a `@handle` is first looked up blindly (D16): the app finds the channel in the catalog without naming it, through a blind evaluation and its group's snapshot, or through the handle index. Only a channel the catalog lacks, or a video link, goes to the server in clear (`POST /add`), which reads it online; the answer comes after at least 8 seconds either way, and the channel enters the catalog at a random time within 6 hours (D16, “Adding a channel”). Meanwhile its row reads “New videos may be late”, with an ⓘ that says why. `/channel/UC…`, feed URLs and ids are also read locally, to refuse an already followed channel without a request. For a video link, the server validates the id (`^[A-Za-z0-9_-]{11}$`), calls oEmbed with a URL rebuilt from that id, then reads the author's page, as for a `@handle`.
- **A name** (“Léna Situations”) is first looked for in the Wikidata dictionary (D16): about 157,000 YouTube channels Wikidata names, with their `@handle`s and names in English and French (labels mul, en, fr: users mostly speak one of them, and other languages' labels only brought names nobody types into the typo search). Each channel also carries a rough audience, one base-36 digit: twice the order of magnitude of its subscriber count as Wikidata gives it (“social media followers” qualified by its channel id), or else 10 for an item with 5 Wikipedia articles or more and 9 for one with fewer, read by a second query per slice; public data, the same for everyone, never anything from the catalog. A slice without it (a copy from before) reads as no audience. Each channel takes the name and `@handle` Wikidata gives for it (qualifiers “named as” and “YouTube handle” of its channel id), and its item's labels and handle only when the item lists no other channel or its handle names this one: an organisation's channels (Apple, Apple Support) would otherwise all show under its name, as twins; one left with nothing of its own is left out. The app downloads all of it (64 slices, about 7 MB) as soon as the user gets ready to add a channel: when the Channels tab's search field takes the focus, which “Add a channel” in the empty feed does too (D12, preparation for the action). It also checks it in the background of a refresh of the feed, after the feed shows as up to date and inside the step the user approved, and the first name looked up downloads it if neither did. Neither the field nor the refresh says so. The field never opens the connection check's dialog: with the check on, it downloads only while an approval is remembered, and otherwise the first name looked up brings it. A refresh checks its version at most once a day (one request) and downloads it again, whole, only when the server has a newer one (by default every 7 days, `WIKIDATA_REFRESH_DAYS`); a failure leaves the copy the device has, and a lookup during a download waits for it. The Privacy page says so. It stays out of backups. It then searches it on the device, in all its names and `@handle`s, accents and case aside; a channel found through a name other than the one its row shows (a translation, a programme's title) shows that name under its own, so the row always holds what was typed, and comes after those found by their shown name: the whole name first, then its start, then every word, and last, as a search engine suggests them (“Did you mean…”, “Similar names”), names a typo or two away (none under 4 letters, one under 8, two beyond; the first letter of each word kept); among channels that match as well, the larger audience comes first, then the shorter name, since a few letters match thousands of obscure channels as well as the famous one typed, and shows up to 10 channels, which are then looked up by their id like a link. Once the device has it, the names that match appear under the field while typing, offline (8 at most), for a `@handle` too. A server without `WIKIDATA_USER_AGENT` has no dictionary. A name is also looked up as the `@handle`s it likely gives (`guessHandles`): its words run together (`lenasituations`), then joined by `.`, `_` or `-`, lowercase and without accents, and last run together with their accents. The app looks them up blindly in the catalog's handle index (D16): one chunk of it for a `@handle` typed, the whole index when a name's guesses fall in several chunks, since the set of chunks read would point to the name. While typing, it does so only once typing pauses and never opens the connection check's dialog (with the check on and nothing approved, the first chip tapped looks them up first). Only the handle the user taps, when the catalog lacks it, goes to the server, which reads its page online, the read counted in the budgets. A channel whose handle doesn't follow its name still needs its link or `@handle`, and the list says so (below).
- **Per-channel menu**: Show videos, types shown (videos / Shorts / lives), Make secret / Stop keeping secret, Remove (with confirmation). Swiping a row also offers Remove.
- **Channels that need attention** come first, in their own group (“Need your attention”), each with the explanation and one button: “Try again” for a channel that couldn't be found online, “Remove” for a channel that no longer exists on YouTube (its videos stay in the feed until then). Swiping offers Remove for both.
- **Avatars** (D4): read by the server when the channel enters the catalog and sent with it; otherwise initials over a blurred picture drawn on the device from the channel's key, the same for a channel before it is followed as after, like the thumbnails' placeholder.
- **`@handle`** kept locally when the server knows it: the one that led to the channel, or else the one oEmbed names as the author of its latest video when it enters the catalog (one request; a channel without videos has none).

## 3. Local password and secret channels (D5)

**Local encryption and lock.** Everything the app keeps on the device is sealed from the first launch, without asking anything (`src/data/vault.ts`). Locking is optional, and one lock serves the app and its secret channels: Face ID, Touch ID, a fingerprint or Windows Hello when the device has it, and always the app's password, which may be a PIN.

- The first launch makes a random key (64 bytes) and seals the database with it, with nothing to choose: welcome, tour, then the app. The key stays on the device (“never”) until the user sets a way to unlock and asks the app to lock. The Lock section of Settings says plainly that the data then opens without asking, guarded by the device's own lock.
- Sealing sits under Dexie (a DBCore middleware): each row of `subscriptions`, `items`, `thumbnails` and `meta` is stored as an XChaCha20-Poly1305 blob, bound to its table and key. Only the fields indexes need stay readable: dates, order of addition and status. Channel and video ids, as keys or index values, become keyed hashes (AES-CMAC), so a copy of the database names no channel and no video; lookups by id stay exact matches. The theme stays readable, for the locked screen. The dictionary (public data) is not sealed.
- The key sits in the `keyring` table, sealed by what the device's passkey gives (WebAuthn PRF: iOS 18.4+ with Passwords, Android with Google Password Manager) and/or by a key stretched from the password (Argon2id, 64 MiB, 2 passes). A passkey whose password manager gives no key (no PRF) locks the app too, as a door: the key then stays on the device, and the app opens it only once the passkey has confirmed the user (`unlockWithKeptKey`). The screen is the same either way: the user can't know which their password manager does, so the app never asks them to. The Privacy page says what this door doesn't stop (a copy of the device's data). The app refuses to lock while nothing would unlock it, so it can never shut the user out of their own data.
- **One passkey per device** (`src/app/BiometricsFlow.tsx`), for unlocking the app and showing secret channels alike. It is made with the same random user id each time on a device (`passkeyUser` in the sealed `meta`, never sent, not in backups), so a password manager replaces it instead of adding one, and named after the device (“Subify on iPhone”). On Android it is made not discoverable (`residentKey: 'discouraged'`), the only way Android keeps it on the phone, out of any password manager: the user sees nothing but their fingerprint or face, and the alert doesn't mention a passkey. An Apple device always saves a synced passkey, whatever a web page asks; Windows Hello keeps it on the computer. Whether Android's gives a key (PRF) decides whether it seals the app's key or guards it as a door. Turning it off keeps its id (`keyring.passkey`): turning it on again uses it, without saving anything new.
- Before the device's sheet, the app says where the key goes, in the device's own words (Passwords built into iOS / macOS, Google Password Manager, Windows Hello): nothing to install. When the device refuses within a second, it showed no sheet: the app opens a guide to turn its password manager on (a web page can't open the system's settings), with “Try again”. A slower refusal is the user closing the sheet, or a passkey the list doesn't show (on an iPhone, one kept in another password manager hides behind “More from…”): the app offers to try again, with that hint, or to make a new passkey, and never forgets one it made. The setting is named after what the user does, “Face ID or Touch ID”, “Touch ID”, “Fingerprint or face”, “Windows Hello”, never “passkey”: what keeps it is the password manager's business.
- **The password is required as soon as something locks**, for when Face ID can't (a deleted passkey, another device): turning on Face ID, “Lock Subify” or “Lock secret channels” asks for it first when there is none. It can be a PIN of 6 digits or more, or any password. As the user types it, the sheet says how long someone holding a copy of the device's data would need to guess it (`src/data/strength.ts`: Argon2id at 64 MiB allows about ten thousand guesses a second on dedicated hardware; common PINs and passwords, runs and repeats are counted as tried first), and suggests a longer one below a month. After 5 wrong passwords, each new attempt waits, twice as long each time (`keyring.attempts`), wherever it is asked. A forgotten password can't be recovered. Asked while the app is open (to show secret channels, or to change it), “Forgot the password?” removes the secret channels, with their videos, then lets the user choose a new one, as a forgotten PIN did: anyone holding the open app could already reset it. At the locked screen, the only way out is to reset the app and restore a backup.
- The Lock section: “Lock Subify” (off: the key stays on the device and the app opens without asking, safe on a locked lost phone only), “Ask to unlock” while it is on (each time the app opens, each time the user comes back to it, after 5, 15 or 60 minutes unused), “Lock secret channels”, the Face ID switch when the device has it, “Choose a password” / “Change the password”, and “Lock now”. Under it, one sentence about what is true now; when Face ID only guards the door (no key from the passkey), it says that a copy of the device's data could still be read, and that turning Face ID off lets the password protect that too. The rows are the same with secret channels or without, so that the screen tells nothing.
- “Lock now” (Settings, Lock) locks the app at once.
- Apps that predate this keep their settings. Face ID without a password still unlocks, and the Lock section asks for a password. A passkey of secret channels' own (`secretCredential`) still works until Face ID is turned on in Settings, which replaces it; a PIN of their own (`secretCodeHash`) until the app's password is chosen or changed, which replaces it.

**Secret channels.**

- Hidden from the feed, the strip and the list (the subscription's `secret` field), on top of the app's lock.
- “Make secret” hides a channel at once, without asking anything. Once the app has a lock, secret channels use it (Face ID first, the password if it doesn't confirm), unless the user turned “Lock secret channels” off, which asks for the lock first: otherwise anyone holding the open app could take it away. Without a lock, showing them asks nothing (they are only out of sight); “Lock secret channels…” in the Channels tab's menu while they show, or the switch in Settings, adds one.
- Double tap on “All”, or “Show secret channels” in the Channels tab's menu. “All” shows in the strip even beside a single channel, so that it doesn't come and go with the secret ones.
- From the second channel, a card in the Channels tab says a channel can be kept secret (“Got it” hides it; a lock already chosen, or a channel made secret, too). The first channel made secret on a device starts a short tour, once: a tip under the Channels tab's ⋯ (“Next: the feed”), then one on “All” in the feed asking for a double tap; once they show without a lock, a last one offers it (“Lock them…”, “Not now”). Each tip can be closed.
- Hidden again as soon as the user goes to another screen of the app, or the app leaves the screen (`visibilitychange`).
- A secret channel can be opened on its own from the list, without revealing the others; it hides again in the same way.
- While they show, or one of them opened on its own, a banner at the top of the Feed and Channels tabs says so, with “Hide”. Each secret video's card, and the secret channel's chip in the feed's strip, have a dashed outline and sit in a halo of smoke that drifts slowly around them (still with reduced motion), grey in light mode and pale in dark mode; in the Channels tab, each secret channel's avatar has the same, its outline a dashed ring. Their text stays as any other's.
- A forgotten PIN of secret channels' own (from an earlier version or a backup) can't be recovered: the secret channels are deleted, with their videos, and the PIN.
- The backup holds the secret status and, from earlier versions, a PIN's hash: a restored device asks for the same PIN. Secret channels that unlocked with the app's lock come back without one (the passkey and the app's password stay on their device): on the restored device they lock like the app, if it is locked. “Restore this backup?” says which of the two the backup's secret channels come back with. A backup made with the app's password needs one of at least 8 characters: it leaves the device, where nothing makes a guesser wait.
- Saving a backup while there are locked secret channels first asks for what shows them, as showing them does, after an alert that says why: a backup opens with the password its maker picks, and anyone holding it and its password can read everything in it.
- Error messages never name a hidden secret channel.

## 4. Settings (Settings tab)

- **Theme** light / dark / system, stored in `meta`. The app's colours (neutral greys, black accent, white in dark mode, red kept for lives, deletions and actions online, white cards on a light grey page) go through Ionic's variables in `theme.css`. Behaviour, sizes, spacing and typography stay Ionic's, per platform (iOS or Material): collapsible large titles on iOS, Ionic popovers for the `⋯` menus, etc.
- **Backup** (D6): a file sealed with a password (Argon2id + XChaCha20-Poly1305) of at least 8 characters: the app's password by default, checked against it, or another one; never stored; date of the last export. The libraries (hash-wasm, @noble/ciphers) load on demand.
- **Restore**: replaces everything, after confirmation. Channels the catalog knows are filled at once from the snapshots of their groups, found blindly (D16), then get the thumbnails of their last 7 days from the same groups (D3); the others go through the submission queue (`POST /add`), one at a time, which carries on by itself, even after a relaunch.
- **Connection check** (D11): off by default, kept in backups.
- **Thumbnails** (D3): how long the device keeps them, 1, 3 or 6 months, a year, or as long as the video, a month by default; each choice shows what it would take for the channels followed (the thumbnails of their videos of the last 30 days, times the average thumbnail, `src/data/thumbnails.ts`). A longer time keeps the new ones longer: a thumbnail deleted doesn't come back, since the device only gets those of the last 7 days. A shorter one deletes the older ones at once, after a confirmation that says how much it frees. Above a month, “Delete older thumbnails…” deletes those older than 1, 3 or 6 months or a year, shorter than the time chosen, without changing it, after the same confirmation. The footer says what thumbnails take on the device. While secret channels are hidden, every number leaves them out. Kept in backups (format version 2; a version 1 backup restores a month).
- **Reset the app**: empties Dexie and the service worker's cache, after confirmation.
- **About**: “Why Subify” (what the app does and doesn't do), the Privacy page (with “Show who runs this server”, which asks the server for its name and operator only when tapped), “Install the app” when the browser allows it, and a message on the server's retention (6 months). A “Contact” link at the bottom, also on the welcome screen before unlocking, opens a form whose message the server emails to its operator, keeping none of it, when its admin turned it on (`MESSAGES_TO`, `apps/server/src/contact.ts`). No server URL in the settings: the app only talks to the server that serves it (D14).

## 5. Platform and safeguards

- **Each server names its app.** The app's build is the same everywhere; the server answers its manifest (`/api/manifest.webmanifest`) and icon links (`/api/icons/…`, redirects to `/icons/red/` or `/icons/blue/`) for the host the request came to: the name its admin set (`INSTANCE_NAME`, required), and the Subify icon in red on `subify.tv`, in blue on any other host, so that a self-hosted app is never mistaken for the official one (`apps/server/src/instance.ts`).

- **Installable PWA.** An invitation to install from the empty feed, then a card in the feed (“Not now” hides it for 7 days; the third time it comes back, it offers “Don’t show again”), and always in Settings. At launch, if the app isn't installed, the browser hasn't promised to keep its data (`storage.persist()`) and a channel was added since the last backup, a toast warns that channels could disappear, at most once a day and never at the same time as the card (`src/data/storage-warning.ts`). Installing goes through the browser's dialog when it offers one (Chromium), otherwise through a step-by-step guide for the browser (Safari and other iOS browsers, Android, Safari on Mac, `src/ui/install.ts`). On iOS, and in Safari on a Mac (“Add to Dock” copies only cookies), the installed app has its own storage, separate from the browser's: the guide says so in a line, and when channels are already followed it takes the user through the move: save a backup here first (the guide reopens once it is saved), install, then restore it in the app. “Learn more” adds that the backup brings secret channels and settings too, and stays a sealed file on the device, with no account and unseen by the server. The feed's card says a backup brings the channels along.
- **Files**: `<input type="file">` to read; to export, a download or file sharing (Web Share).
- **Every network call is declared.** On the client, a single `api` module (`credentials: 'omit'`, `referrerPolicy: 'no-referrer'`). On the server, a single injected `Fetcher`, which only accepts URLs rebuilt by the source adapter. Tested.
- **Polling rate**: inside the pass, one channel at a time with a random gap (up to 2 s), a cap per pass (`POLL_BATCH_SIZE` per minute of its period), ±10 % jitter, and backoff after any failure (30 min, doubling up to a day). A channel without items (one added while its feeds failed) is first read again at the next three passes, then backs off the same way; when it enters, a failed read is tried once more a second later, within the 8 seconds the answer waits anyway, through its channel feed alone (its typed playlists only if that feed lists something), and only once its page confirmed it exists. A feed that answers an empty list for such a channel counts as a failure too (YouTube sometimes does that for channels with uploads), for the first six reads in a row after the two at its addition, about four hours; after that the channel is taken for one with no uploads, polled as usual, and shown as such.
- **Source adapter**: everything YouTube-specific (link resolution, which feeds to read, classification) lives behind an interface, so that other platforms can be added.
- **Classification** by the `UULF` / `UUSH` / `UULV` playlists, falling back on the channel feed; lives win when an id appears in several feeds; an id is inserted only once. In the fallback, a `/shorts/` link in the channel feed is enough to classify it as `short`, otherwise `unknown`.
- **A known item keeps its type and date**; title and thumbnail are updated. Exception: an `unknown` item can be reclassified. Every real change reaches devices through the next pass's batch of the stream.
- **Fixtures**: real recorded feeds (`fixtures:record`). No test contacts YouTube. A real feed already shows a trap: `<yt:channelId>` is written there without the `UC` prefix.
- **Language** (D8): interface in English, neutral wording (“the platform”) except “Open in YouTube”, typographic punctuation. Code, comments, commits and documents in English.
- **Dependencies**: pnpm, a 7-day `minimumReleaseAge`, install scripts forbidden except for a minimal list (`better-sqlite3`, `esbuild`, `workerd`), Dependabot, a weekly audit.

## Limits

Subify only reads what the platform publishes for everyone: the Atom feeds of channels and their playlists, oEmbed, and a channel's public page. What these data don't contain, the app doesn't show:

- **Video durations, start times of upcoming lives**: missing from the Atom feeds.
- **Searching channels by name**: Subify itself never searches YouTube. A name is found in the Wikidata dictionary on the device, as the `@handle`s it likely gives; otherwise the app asks for a link or a `@handle`.
- **Videos older** than the latest 15 of each feed, when a channel is added. After that, the server follows the channel continuously and misses nothing.
- **Background refresh**: no reliable equivalent in a PWA, and no need for one: the server follows the channels continuously, the app only has to download when asked.

## Decisions

### D10: what the server sees, and what it keeps

A server that knew nothing would send everyone the same answers: the changes of the whole catalog, and enough to recognise one's channels without naming them. Each device would then download data in proportion to the size of the catalog, not to its subscriptions. Subify stands between the two (D16):

- **Adding a channel the catalog has**, or **restoring a backup**: the device finds each channel blindly (a blind evaluation, then the snapshot of its group, about 40 channels), so the server learns which groups it read, never which channels.
- **Adding a channel the catalog lacks**, or **by a video link**: the PWA sends the link (`POST /add`). The server reads the channel online and answers with it, its latest videos (at most 15 per type, plus any upcoming ones) and the thumbnails of its last 7 days. It sees which channel is added, as before.
- **Staying up to date**: the device downloads the stream's batches of its groups, every entry padded to the same size, and opens only its own channels' entries.

What stays guaranteed: no account, no identifier, no cookie; the server stores nothing about users (a test checks that the read routes write nothing to the database); the server's code logs no IP, URL or list of channels, and no log shows which channels anyone follows; no IP header read. Anyone who doesn't want to trust the official instance hosts their own and installs the app from it (D14).

### D11: the connection check, the only exception to “no IP read”

Before going online, the user may want to check which country their connection appears from, for example to make sure their VPN is on. The device only talks to the server: so the server answers, and the country it sees is what matters, since it is the one that sees the IP.

- `GET /api/connection` answers `{ v: 2, country: "CH" | null }`. On Cloudflare, the country comes from `request.cf.country`, which Cloudflare computes anyway: no extra service or GeoIP database. Only the Worker entry reads it, and only when this route calls for it (a lazy function passed in the bindings); it is neither stored nor logged. On Node (self-hosting), there is no country: the answer is `null` and the app says it can't tell.
- Tests guarantee it: only this route reads the country and it writes nothing to the database (`test/shared/connection.test.ts`); only `src/entry/worker.ts` touches the request's `cf` properties (`test/node/privacy-source.test.ts`).
- On the client, every step that goes online passes through `ConnectionGate` (`src/data/connection-check.ts`), and each `Api` refuses a request made outside an approved step. With the check on, a refresh or an addition shows the dialog; the submission queue only moves on while an approval is remembered, and never opens the dialog itself.
- The remembered approval (minutes, an hour, a day, until the app is closed) stays on the device, never in a backup, and is forgotten when the setting changes or a backup is restored.

### D15: seeding the catalog

A catalog holding only the channels users added would say too much: anyone holding its key, starting with the server's operator, would know that at least one person follows each. Asking whether a channel is in it is also possible (D16, “Accepted limit”). For a small channel, that would come close to pointing at someone. So the server seeds its catalog with channels it adds itself, and a channel being there no longer tells whether someone follows it.

- **What the server does.** A scheduled task (`src/catalog/seeding.ts`, every minute) adds a channel now and then, `SEEDED_CHANNELS_PER_DAY` a day on average. Seeding is off by default (0): the official instance turns it on (24 a day), and a self-hosted catalog fills only with its users' channels unless its admin sets it. It goes through the same code as a user (`submitInput`): the same page read, the same budgets, the same update waiting for the pass, the same slot. Nothing stored and no log tells a seeded channel apart.
- **Two public sources, no list in the code.** Seeded channels are drawn from the channels the catalog cites (below), and from the Wikidata dictionary (D16), a quarter of them, and all of them while no cited channel is ripe. A list in the code would be public, so anyone could set its channels aside; a channel drawn in secret among the dictionary's 157,000 can't be. Dictionary channels are drawn alike, whatever their audience: favouring famous ones would leave the little-known ones uncited and seeded by nobody. They need no wait, as nothing ties them to a date, so they cover a new catalog from its first users on, and their videos bring the first citations. Without a dictionary (no `WIKIDATA_USER_AGENT`), only cited channels are seeded.
- **Channels the catalog cites.** These are the channels cited in the descriptions of the catalog's videos (`/channel/UC…`, `/@handle`, `/c/`, `/user/` links, at most three per channel read, those whose newest citation is oldest first), read from the feeds already downloaded. They wait in the seed pool, 64 encrypted rows of a fixed size rewritten at every pass, each with the newest video citing it. A candidate is drawn only once that video has left its channel's public feeds, which list its latest 15 videos and the latest 15 of each type: the pass sees it in the channel's slot, when 15 newer videos of its type came or it was deleted, or when the slot no longer holds it (past `RETENTION_DAYS`). Seeded from outside, a channel can then no longer be traced to a citation still in the feeds. A channel's oldest videos leave first: a new catalog's first candidates ripen within days, and a citation by a channel posting once a day waits about 15 days, once a week about 15 weeks. A channel cited again by a newer video waits for that one instead; a candidate kept from before this rule (pool version 1) waits 30 days after it was first cited. Candidates cited once come first. Every channel entering, by a user or by the server, erases the candidates that lead to it: a candidate left next to its channel would tell that a user added it. A link that leads to no channel is forgotten.
- **When.** The pass counts users' additions by hour of day, in 24 counters halved every week: seeded channels enter at the hours users add at, whatever their time zone, without any date kept per channel.
- **How many.** Removing seeded channels later would set them apart. So seeding slows down as the catalog fills, and stops at half its capacity (900 channels by default): the rest is for users.
- **Cost.** A seeded channel costs what an addition costs: one page read, up to four feeds, an avatar, the thumbnails of its last 7 days and, without a known `@handle`, one oEmbed request, then polling.
- **Limits.** Seeding starts from the channels users added: a new catalog's first channels are its users', and seeding protects them only as others join them. Waiting for the citing video to leave the feeds hides a citation from someone who reads them afterwards, not from someone who archives the feeds of the catalog's channels all along, YouTube itself among them: to them, a channel nothing cites is likely a user's. Dictionary channels blur that only for channels the dictionary has. With as many seeded channels as users' channels, an uncited channel of the dictionary is a user's about 59 % of the time (92 % without dictionary seeds), 12 % with ten times more seeded channels (54 %); an uncited channel outside it, often a small one, about 87 %, and 40 % with ten times more seeded channels (33 % with cited ones only, since dictionary seeds take a quarter of the room). These are estimates, with 90 % of channels seeded from citations and 30 % of users' channels cited, half of users' channels in the dictionary and a quarter of seeded ones from it. The more the catalog grows, the more channels it cites.
- **Principles.** A seeded channel is read like any channel: its public page once, when it enters the catalog, then its feeds. The server reads no page to find channels to add: the dictionary is the one it already downloads for searches by name.

### D9: hosting on Workers' Paid plan

Limits, from [developers.cloudflare.com](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/): on the Paid plan ($5 a month), 30 s of CPU per Cron Trigger that runs more often than hourly (time waiting on the network or D1 doesn't count), 15 minutes of wall time, 1,000 D1 queries per invocation (a `db.batch()` counts as one), 50 M D1 rows written a month included, 10 GB per database.

- **The pass** (D16) reads and rewrites every row of the catalog, in batches of 50: at 2,500 slots, about 10.8 M rows written a month. Its period is 10 minutes, and grows once the table would write more than about 45 M rows a month (past about 10,400 rows a pass, `src/catalog/sizing.ts`).
- **Polling inside the pass**: `POLL_BATCH_SIZE` (5 by default) channels per minute of the period, spread over the pass. The same value is the default on Node, and a self-hoster can raise it.
- **No conditional requests at YouTube**: its feeds send neither `ETag` nor `Last-Modified`. The adaptive rate, reading only the channel feed when nothing is new, and WebSub are what limit the volume.
- **CPU**: thrifty Atom parsing (one pass, no DOM); streamed read of a `@handle` page, stopped as soon as the id, the title and the avatar are found, instead of loading 1 MB of HTML. Blind evaluations go by batches of 10 or 50, limited by `BLIND_BATCHES_PER_MINUTE`.
- **Between passes, nothing is written to D1** but the dictionary refresh: updates and the shared budgets wait in a Durable Object (`COORDINATOR`), in memory on Node.
- **WebSub**: the notification answers right away; reading the feeds by type fits in `waitUntil` (under 30 s, 4 subrequests), and what it learnt waits for the pass.
- **Rate limiting**: on Workers, request rates can be limited at the edge, in Cloudflare's dashboard, without the app seeing addresses.

### D16: a catalog the server stores without reading it

The README calls this design, seeding included (D15), **Sealring**, after the seal ring that closed letters: the sync stream's ring of sealed batches is part of it.

A readable catalog would tell anyone holding a copy of the database (a dump, a restore of an earlier state, a legal request) its channels, their videos, and roughly when each channel entered it. So the server stores the catalog without being able to read it at rest; who follows what is never stored at all.

**Goals.** The database alone reveals nothing, even across many copies over time: not which channels, not how many beyond a coarse size class, not their order of arrival, not what changed or when. With the server's secret, the catalog is readable, but never who follows what, which channels were seeded, or when one entered. Through the API, without the secret, nobody can list or download the catalog.

**Out of scope**: an operator who serves modified code; the hosting platform reading the server's memory; account-level metrics (request and query counts); the WebSub hub seeing which channels the server follows; IP addresses (a VPN, which D11 checks).

**Keys.** One master secret (`MASTER_SECRET`: a Workers secret, or a generated file on Node, `src/node/secret.ts`), never in the database. HKDF derives from it an AES-256-GCM key for every row and a VOPRF key (RFC 9497, ristretto255-SHA512). For a channel, `O = VOPRF("<source>:<id>")`: its slot id, its transit key `K_c` and its group are derived from O. A device gets O by blind evaluation: the server computes it without learning the channel. Losing the secret makes the catalog unreadable; devices then add their channels back by themselves.

**What the database holds** (`migrations/0001_slots.sql`). Every table is `WITHOUT ROWID`, keyed by pseudorandom ids or fixed positions, every blob of a table the same size; no plaintext column, no per-channel date. On Node, the SQLite file is rewritten once a day so that its layout keeps no trace of the order of writes. D1 keeps 30 days of Time Travel, which can't be turned off: every restorable state is as unreadable as the current one.

- **Slots**: one channel or one decoy per row, 40 KB: the channel, its schedule (next poll, failures, WebSub state, its secret and callback token, days “not found”) and its items (at most 400, none older than `RETENTION_DAYS`, those of the last 7 days with their thumbnail's ThumbHash; the oldest go first when titles don't fit, so a busy channel with long CJK or emoji titles keeps about 70 to 90).
- **Size class**: the table starts at 2,500 slots and grows by ×1.5 when half of it is taken, at a time drawn within the 3 days after: a growth step tells only that the catalog, seeded channels included, crossed that size.
- **Other rows**, all rewritten by the pass: the catalog's state, the seed pool and the hour counters (D15), the thumbnail ring and the thumbnail lots (D3), the stream's batches, whose video entries carry each thumbnail's ThumbHash, the handle index. The Wikidata dictionary is public data, kept in clear.

**The pass** (`src/catalog/pass.ts`). Every 10 minutes or more (D9), it reads every slot and rewrites it with a fresh nonce, changed or not, applying on the way what waits in the coordinator and polling the channels due. Between passes nothing is written: an addition, a WebSub notification, the budgets wait in the coordinator, sealed.

**Staying up to date: the sync stream.** Each pass publishes one batch: for each group, the same number of 512-byte entries, real changes sealed under `K_c` behind a tag that changes at every pass, and random entries for the rest, with a ×3 margin over the expected changes (changes without room wait for the next pass). A device downloads its groups' entries whole and keeps those its channels' tags match: the server learns which groups a device reads (about 40 channels each), nothing finer, and from the cursor it sends only the 12 hours its last refresh fell in. The stream keeps 30 days; a device away longer reads its groups' snapshots.

**Adding a channel.** A channel the catalog has is found blindly: by its id (up to 50 per evaluation request), or by its `@handle` in the handle index (one chunk for a typed handle, the whole index for a name whose guessed handles fall in several chunks). The device then reads its group's snapshot, computed on request: every slot of the group, each channel sealed under its own key, then the group's thumbnails of the last 7 days (D3). A channel the catalog lacks, a video link (there is no index of videos), and a handle entered since the last hourly rebuild of the index go the clear way, `POST /add`, alone in their request; the answer has a fixed size and comes after at least 8 seconds, known channel or not, and the channel gets its slot at the first pass after a time drawn within 6 hours (`ENTRY_DELAY`), every channel new to the catalog alike, seeded or not: at the next pass, the channels one person adds together, a restore's especially, would enter together, and when one enters would tell when someone added it, to anyone who saw them use the app. The update waits in the coordinator under its entry time, and a pass takes only those due. The device that added it has it at once, with its latest videos; those published meanwhile come at the first poll after it enters, so they may be a few hours late, and the app says so on its row for 6 hours (it can't tell whether the catalog had it, so it says so after any addition in clear). The same channel added meanwhile from another device isn't found blindly and goes the clear way again. Estimates, with 24 seeded channels a day: a single addition, tied to the time its user was seen online, is the channel entering next about 81 % of the time at 10 users' additions a day without the delay, 11 % with it (54 % and 3 % at 100 a day); a restore's 10 uncommon channels make 98 % of the entries of their window without it, 54 % with it (92 % and 24 % at 100 a day). It does nothing against the host, which sees the address and the request together (D10). On Node, the coordinator is the process's memory: a restart loses the channels still waiting, which the safety net sends again. Once the entry time is past by an hour, a refresh checks the snapshots, and a channel still missing goes back to the import queue (the safety net). A restored backup reads the snapshots of its groups once, about 40 MB at the start, then their thumbnails, about as much again, instead of a week of lots.

**Accepted limit: membership tests.** Anyone can test whether a guessed channel is in the catalog, through a blind evaluation and a snapshot. That is the price of finding known channels without naming them. The protection is seeding (D15): being there doesn't mean being followed. Evaluations and snapshots are rate-limited (`BLIND_BATCHES_PER_MINUTE`, 120 snapshots a minute).

**Dead channels.** A channel is removed one way only: its channel feed answers “not found” on 30 consecutive days, each day counted only when other channels' feeds answered in the same pass (YouTube's feeds have site-wide outages of hours to days), and a read of its `/channel/` page confirms it is gone. Its slot becomes a decoy under a new id at the next pass, and the stream tells devices: the app shows it under “Need your attention” with “Remove”.

**Search by name.** In this order: the Wikidata dictionary, downloaded whole to the device and searched there (the server never sees a name it matches); guessed handles in the handle index, blindly; last, the guessed handles online.

## Design notes

1. **API under `/api`** and a **versioned answer format** (`v`), unknown parameters and fields refused.
2. **Streamed page read**: a channel's page weighs about 2 MB and what is needed (id, title, avatar) is around 820 KB in; the read stops there. It carries a fixed consent cookie (`SOCS`), the same for every request, which identifies no one.
3. **Client-side retention**: the app keeps its local history beyond the server's 6 months. The “limited history” message shows when subscribing to a channel for which the server only has recent items.
4. **Fixed sizes everywhere** (D16): every stored blob and every answer that could say something about the catalog has a size set by the code, never by the content; codecs in `packages/shared/src/codec/` pad or cut by a documented rule.
